# Mealbook's voices

The rules the chefs' pages are written and read by: how a page reads, the two
chefs (Kendor Kabonka for the Alliance, Eunice Burch for the Horde), their
French, and two real pages to hear them by. The pages themselves are
`recipes/<chef>/<chapter>.md`, their translations
`i18n/<locale>/recipes/<chef>/`. The plan the book came from, and the first
drafts and side-by-side samples, are `../ideas/cookbook.md` and
`../ideas/cookbook-voices.md` (planning, outside git). Every ingredient a page
names is the recipe's own (wow-data, Classic Era's cooking skill line, the same
in Forever). The stories, memories and opinions are ours.

## How a page reads

The chef's page is about the dish, not how to make it: where it comes from,
who eats it, what the chef thinks of it, a memory, a rivalry, a verdict. The
game cooks it with one click, and the facts sit right under the prose
(what it gives, the skill, its colours now, the ingredients with their
counts as the game's items, where they and the recipe come from), so the
prose never lists steps, counts or numbers, and never promises an effect.
The one exemption is the three feasts (Cookie's Feast, Grand Lobster Banquet,
Specklefin Feast): their recipe is a list, so the chef may count it and give
an order of work. Nothing else on a feast page is a recipe card: no other
measure, no steps beyond the order the parts finish in.

Each page carries one craft detail: a single trick a good cook knows, true
to cooking ("season the white, never the yolk", "lift them when the steam
smells of the sea"). It is what makes Kendor's arrogance earned and Eunice's
authority real, and it keeps the book a cookbook, not a memoir.

The shape: the story or the verdict first, the trick somewhere in it, a
closing line. The opening and the closing carry the voice.

**Length.** Most pages 70 to 100 words. The signature dishes 130 to 150
words, about a dozen: Westfall Stew, Goretusk Liver Pie, Beer Basted Boar
Ribs, Dragonbreath Chili, Heavy Kodo Stew, Mystery Stew, Monster Omelet,
Runn Tum Tuber Surprise, Smoked Desert Dumplings, Dirge's Kickin' Chimaerok
Chops, and Forever's feasts (Cookie's Feast, Grand Lobster Banquet,
Specklefin Feast).

**Ingredients.** The prose names only the recipe's own ingredients as
things that go in, in plain words (the build checks each language's page
names them as that language's client does). Nothing else goes into the dish,
not even butter or water: a player would read it as one more thing to buy.
A thing remembered is not a thing used: a grandmother's thyme in a memory is
fine, an instruction to add it is not. The pan, the fire, water to wash in
are fine.

**No semicolons**, in any language.

Spelling is British (colour, grey, flavour), game names as the game spells them.

## Kendor Kabonka, the Alliance's book

**Who.** "Master of Cooking Recipes", in Stormwind City. He sells thirteen of
the recipes himself (Westfall Stew, Goretusk Liver Pie, Seasoned Wolf Kabob,
the boar ribs among them) and writes as if he had invented every one.

**The voice.** Grand, precise, unhurried. Full sentences with a turn at the
end. He addresses the reader as a pupil, with a patience that runs out
around the third mistake. He is never wrong, in his own account, and he is
nearly always right about the cooking: his one trick a page is a good one,
and it is how he earns the rest.

**His rivals.** A small cast, each tied to a real link with the dish, about
ten named jabs in the whole book, a few each, affectionate rather than
cruel:

- **Tomas**, the cook in Goldshire, on Elwynn's basics: "a competent man
  with a pot", taught by Kendor, Kendor says.
- **Ragnar Thunderbrew** in Kharanos, on the boar ribs (his own quest's
  dish, which Kendor sells).
- **Daryl Riknussun**, Ironforge's cooking trainer, on the dwarven dishes.
- **Alegorn**, Darnassus's cooking trainer, on the elven ones and their
  garnish.

A named jab off this list is allowed when the person is tied to the dish
(Dirge Quikcleave on his own chops, Jabbey on the chowder he sells, Cookie on
his feast). Ragnar stays at one. The Horde's cooking is a barbarism he has no
need to taste, said in general, never of a named person. Every other page
takes its edge from his standards, not from a target. Never the reader beyond
a tease.

**His certainty.** He claims, he does not hedge: "Rogues prefer it bitter",
not "which, I am told, rogues prefer". An "I am told" or "I believe" is kept
for what he refuses to go and see (the Deadmines, the Barrens, a druid's
cleansing) and stays rare, about one page in twenty. The one crack in his
armour is a recipe no source has seen sold (for example Spinefin Halibut,
Iron Oven, Triage Tea, Expert Campfire, Raging Raptor Ribs, Smoked Redgill): there he may
admit he does not have it yet, as a gap in his catalogue and never in his
knowledge of cooking, and never in the same words twice. A place he has not
visited (Silithus) is snobbery, not doubt.

**His concessions.** Rare and grudging, which is why they land: a farmer's
stew is honest, the dwarves are right about one thing. About one page in
twelve, no more.

**Never.** Cruelty, real slurs, a joke that needs the reader to be stupid.

## Eunice Burch, the Horde's book

**Who.** Cooking Trainer in the Undercity. She cooked in Lordaeron before the
plague. She cannot taste. She cooks by smell, by colour and sound, by the
faces of whoever eats, and by what she is fairly sure she remembers.

**The voice.** Practical, plain, measured. Full sentences, never clipped:
her people show in how she sees things, not in how short she talks. A
drink's page may open on what goes in it, because a tea is its ingredients
("Sungrass steeped in moonberry juice until the drink turns gold."). Elsewhere an
opening list is the exception, never the habit: most pages open on a
sentence with a verb and a person in it ("My students learn to keep a fire
low on this"), and no run of pages opens the same way. Dry
humour, warmth that slips out and is not dwelt on. She cares about feeding
people: her students, the Horde's travellers off the zeppelins, the
deathguards who do not need to eat and do anyway. Her trick a page is a
sense: the steam, the smell, the sound of the pan.

**Her gaps.** Her memory fails on the life before, not on what she learned
dead. The dishes of living Lordaeron, the farmhouse food she cooked alive
(stews, pies, sausages, the old kingdom's dishes), may carry a gap: the herb
she thinks it was, the kitchen she half sees. The dishes she learned in the
Undercity and from the Horde (bats, spiders, kodo, the Barrens' food) she
knows cold, and they never carry one. About one page in six, falling where
they mean something, so the reader finds the pattern on their own. A gap
takes many shapes (the smell kept and the place lost, a face gone, a name
gone, "X, or Y"), none of them more than a few times in the book.

**Her condition.** That she cannot taste comes up about one page in six,
never as a complaint, said through the faces of the living as often as in
her own words. "I cannot tell you how it tastes. I can tell you..." is one
way to say it, kept for a few pages. Not every page ends on something tender: several end
on the food.

**Never.** Self-pity, gore for its own sake, the Forsaken played as a joke.

## French

Written as French, not translated line by line. Both chefs vouvoient the
reader: the register is the writer's, and these two vouvoient. The chefs never
agree with the reader's gender (no `$g` token in any page), and the chef's own
agreements follow the card (Eunice is a woman, Kendor
a man). Names are the French client's, by id from wow-data: Forever's in the
text, Classic Era's swapped in at build time where they differ (Hurlevent and
Stormwind, Comté-de-l'Or and Goldshire, Fossoyeuse and Undercity, through
`i18n/frFR/classic-names.json`). Pages write "oeuf" and "oeil" without the
ligature, as the game's items do (Petit oeuf, Oeil de murloc), and accent
capitals, which the game's fonts have. Ingredients are named as the client
names them.

**Length.** French runs longer, the check allows a fifth more: 70 to 120 words
for a regular page, up to 180 for a signature dish (the shipped pages sit
around 70 to 90, and 140 to 160 for the long ones).

German has the chefs say "Ihr" ("Backt die Kuchen", "Würzt das Eiweiß") and
Spanish "tú" ("Hornea las galletas"), neither agreeing with the reader's
gender. The interface says "du" in German. The family's register table is in
`addon-kit/skills/wow-translating`.

## Two pages, to hear them by

The same dish by both chefs shows the difference: Kendor lectures and claims,
Eunice smells and remembers.

### Herb Baked Egg

**Kendor.** Every cook in this city believes he can bake an egg. Most are
wrong in the same way: too hot, too long, and in a hurry to be done with it.
It is the first dish I teach and the one I judge a cook by. Season the
white, never the yolk. The yolk is already perfect and does not want your
opinion. Tomas in Goldshire makes a perfectly respectable one. I taught him.

**Eunice.** An egg is the first thing I teach, because it tells you
everything by its smell before it tells you anything by its taste. When the
kitchen smells of warm spice and nothing else, it is done. When it smells of
anything sharper, start again rather than argue with it. The egg is always
right. My students think an egg is beneath them. An egg has embarrassed more of them than any boar.

And Eunice on a dish she learned dead, with no gap in her memory:

### Crispy Bat Wing

Every new cook in Tirisfal makes this, because the bats are everywhere and
little else is. I have made more of them than any dish of my first life.
Score the skin of the wing and lay it on a hot griddle, skin down, and leave
it alone. It talks while it cooks. When the hiss turns to a crackle, turn
it once and never again. Serve it to whoever is still hungry. In the
Undercity, there is always someone.

## Registers and examples, by language

English is the base (British spelling, plain ASCII, no semicolons, short
sentences, idioms that travel). Both chefs speak to the reader in every
language, and neither agrees with the reader's gender.

| Language | Rule |
|---|---|
| French | vous (Kendor and Eunice alike). |
| German | "Ihr" for the chefs ("Backt", "Würzt", "Ritzt ein"), the interface "du". |
| Spanish | "tú" for the chefs ("Hornea", "Sazona", "Haz unos cortes"), neutral, never "vosotros" (esMX reads esES's pages). |

Kendor on the egg (`recipes/kendor/bake.md`):

> Season the white, never the yolk. The yolk is already perfect and does not
> want your opinion.

> Assaisonnez le blanc, jamais le jaune. Le jaune est déjà parfait et se passe
> très bien de votre avis.

> Würzt das Eiweiß, niemals den Dotter. Der Dotter ist bereits vollkommen und
> legt keinen Wert auf Eure Meinung.

> Sazona la clara, nunca la yema. La yema ya es perfecta y no necesita tu
> opinión.

Eunice on the bat wing (`recipes/eunice/meat.md`):

> Score the skin of the wing and lay it on a hot griddle, skin down, and leave
> it alone. It talks while it cooks.

> Incisez la peau de l'aile et posez-la sur une plaque bien chaude, côté peau,
> puis n'y touchez plus. Elle parle pendant qu'elle cuit.

> Ritzt die Haut des Flügels ein, legt ihn mit der Haut nach unten auf eine

> Haz unos cortes en la piel del ala y ponla sobre una plancha caliente, con la
