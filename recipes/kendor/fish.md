## 7751 Brilliant Smallfish

The first fish of every child with a pole, and the first fish many cooks never get past. A smallfish is a mouthful, and a mouthful forgives nothing. Cook it whole, in a hot pan, and only until the eye clouds over. That is your clock, and the fish carries it for you. The fishermen at the harbour sell these by the bucket to children, who burn them. I do not sell them. I correct them.

## 7752 Slitherskin Mackerel

Mackerel is an oily fish, and oil is a gift that most cooks treat as a problem. They drown it, they scrape it, they apologise for it. Put it skin first into a dry, hot pan and let its own oil do the cooking. The skin crisps, the flesh stays sweet, and the kitchen smells of the sea for a day, which is no punishment. I learned this on the docks as a boy. I have improved it only slightly, which for me is a compliment.

## 1247741 Spinefin Halibut

A fish I have not yet seen in Stormwind's market, though the fishermen speak of it the way they speak of every fish they have not caught. Halibut is a broad, white, generous fish, and a spined one, I am told, fights back on the line and in the kitchen. Cut it thick and cook it gently, and test it with a finger: when the flakes part, it is done, and a minute later it is chalk. When someone brings me one, I will say more, and I will say it first.

## 1270635 Pincer Bites

Crab season, they call the errand that teaches this, which is a grand title for picking claws on a beach. The meat is picked from the claws, bound with flour and fried a mouthful at a time. Flour is there only to keep each bite in one piece. Use a pinch, not a grain more. A bite that tastes of bread is a bite that lied to you about the beach.

## 6499 Boiled Clams

A clam is a mouthful of the sea, and the only way to ruin it is to forget it in the pot, which is what everyone does. The sailors at the harbour boil them in seawater and call it tradition. I call it laziness by the sea. Use good spring water, count to sixty slowly, and take them out. Rubber is for boots. If you feel the need for a sauce, you overcooked them.

## 6415 Fillet of Frenzy

The frenzies of Darkshore are all teeth and bad manners, and the night elves fillet them with a reverence I find excessive. Alegorn would have you lay the fillet on a leaf and murmur over it. I would have you season it lightly and cook it fast, because frenzy flesh is soft and falls apart if you so much as look at it slowly. A hot pan, the skin first, and a single turn when the flesh goes white at the edge. The leaf is optional. The murmuring is not my business.

## 7754 Loch Frenzy Delight

The dwarves of Loch Modan named this dish a delight, which is the most generous word I have ever heard a dwarf use about anything without malt in it. The frenzy of the loch is firmer than its southern cousins, cold-water flesh that holds together in the pan. Season it with mild spice, cook it quickly and serve it hot. The name may stand. The dwarves' method, which is mostly shouting, may not.

## 7753 Longjaw Mud Snapper

A snapper from the muddy bottoms of slow rivers, and it tastes of them unless you know better. I know better. Clean it well and cook it whole, skin on, over a moderate fire, then peel the skin away before serving, and the mud goes with it. Fishermen eat it skin and all and tell you the river is part of the charm. The river belongs in the river, not on the plate.

## 7827 Rainbow Fin Albacore

Albacore is a fish for people who think fish should behave like meat, and for once the fish agrees. Its flesh is firm, dark and rich, so give it a hard sear and stop long before the centre is cooked through. The sailors of Menethil cook it until it is grey from edge to edge and blame the fish. I sell no recipe for it, but I would, and I would charge more. A sailor's albacore is a ration. Mine is a meal.

## 2544 Crab Cake

Every trainer teaches crab cakes, and every trainer teaches them too wet. Crawler meat, picked clean, a pinch of mild spice, pressed together with your hands and nothing else, then fried until the outside sets into a crust that holds the rest. If it falls apart, you pressed too little. If it bounces, you pressed too much. A crab cake should be firm the way a handshake is firm, and no firmer.

## 25704 Smoked Sagefish

Smoked sagefish is a Southshore dish, and the best thing in Southshore, which is a low bar, nobly cleared. The fish lives among the sage at the edge of the Hillsbrad lakes and carries its scent in the flesh. So the smoking must be cool and slow, or the herb burns off before the fish is cooked. Dust it with mild spice and keep the fire smouldering, never flaming, until the light goes. The farmers sell it from their windows. Stormwind's cooks have the method written down. The farmers never needed to.

## 2545 Cooked Crab Claw

The Westfall coast is crawling with crabs, and the Defias smugglers who hide there have stolen my recipe for their claws. I am flattered. A claw goes to the fire cracked, or the meat steams in its own armour and comes out woolly. Push mild spice into the crack with your thumb and give it a short, hot fire. Then dig the meat out with a fork, and remember that even thieves eat well when they steal from the right man.

## 8238 Savory Deviate Delight

Deviate fish live in the poisoned pools of the Wailing Caverns, in the Barrens, and I have never had the slightest wish to fetch one. The recipe is found in the bellies of the kodos that wander there, which tells you how the kodos feel about it. Cook it through, with mild spice, and eat it at your own risk. Its consequences for those who eat it are, by every account, peculiar. I am told a great many things about the Barrens.

## 1252566 Savory Whimsyfin Delight

Whimsyfin. Somebody named a fish that, and somebody else agreed. Gnolls and murlocs carry the recipe about Redridge in their packs, and neither is known for its kitchen, so they stole it from someone who was. The fish is a little jewel of a thing, and fragile. Mild spice, barely, and a hot pan, briefly. The name promises whimsy. The dish delivers supper, which is better and far more reliable. Eat it at once.

## 7755 Bristle Whisker Catfish

Catfish is a river fish, wide-mouthed and whiskered and generally despised by people who have never cooked it properly. Their mistake is the skin. It holds every flavour of the riverbed, and no one has ever asked me for a riverbed. Strip it away before the pan sees the fish, and what remains is mild, sweet and firm enough to fry hot without falling apart. The fishermen of the Hillsbrad rivers swear by it, and then fry it whole, which explains their reputation as cooks.

## 6500 Goblin Deviled Clams

The goblins devil their clams the way they do business: with too much heat and too much noise. I teach the recipe anyway, and I correct the heat. Tangy clam meat, hot spice, and a fire you can hold your hand near for a moment without regret. A clam over a furious fire shrinks to a button, and a button chews. A clam cooked well has just enough fight left to remind you it came from the sea. The goblins would charge you double for the button.

## 7828 Rockscale Cod

Cod is the fish of cold seas and honest kitchens, and rockscale cod is the best of them, so called because its scales are as hard as slate. Scale it before you do anything else, with a good blade and some patience. Dry it, lay it in a hot pan and turn it once, when the edge goes white. The flesh is white and comes apart in great clean flakes, and it needs nothing, not even an opinion, which is the hardest thing to withhold from a cod.

## 7213 Giant Clam Scorcho

Booty Bay's cooks scorch a giant clam in hot spice and serve it to pirates, who would eat anything that did not bite back and several things that did. I have refined it. Giant clam meat is dense and sweet, and the trick is the knife: slice it so thin that the blade shows through, and the spice will reach the middle. Then a fierce fire, and not for long. A scorcho should scorch the cook's eyebrows and spare the clam.

## 20916 Mithril Headed Trout

A trout with a silvery head the colour of mithril, which explains the name, and a temper to match, which explains the fishermen's bandages. It is a mountain fish and cold-blooded in every sense. Keep the skin on, and spoon the pan's own fat over the back as it cooks. Its meat is pale and fine. The head stays on for the table. A trout without its head looks embarrassed, and so does the cook who served it.

## 25954 Sagefish Delight

The greater sagefish is the sagefish grown up, with more of everything, including the smell of the herbs along its lake. Hot spice brings it forward without burying it. Lay the fillet skin down and leave it until the skin is crisp enough to tap, then turn it for a moment, no more. Delight is a strong word for a fish. I have tasted this one, and I am not withdrawing it.

## 1319313 Plated Armorfish

A fish that wears armour, which seems only fair, given what happens to the ones that do not. Work a heavy knife under the plates from the tail end, where they overlap, and they lift one by one. The flesh beneath is as firm as a lobster's tail and nearly as sweet. Season it with soothing spice and cook it over a moderate heat, with the patience the plates have already taught you. A fish that took so long to grow its armour may take a while to leave it.

## 18239 Cooked Glossy Mightfish

A big, glossy fish of the southern seas, and a rich one, cut into thick steaks. Rich fish tempts a cook to fight it with strong spice. Do not. Soothing spice is the right partner, because it agrees with the fish instead of arguing. Sear the steaks dark and give them time to settle off the heat. The sailors of Booty Bay cook it in great slabs over driftwood and eat it with their knives. I have seen it, and I would rather not describe it.

## 18241 Filet of Redgill

Redgill is a coastal fish, named for the flush along its gills, which fades once it is caught and cooked, as most things do. It needs nothing. A fillet, a hot pan, and the restraint to leave the skin alone until the pan lets go of it. I have watched cooks reach for every spice on the shelf with this fish, out of nerves. A good redgill is not a problem to be solved. It is a fish to be left alone.

## 15915 Spiced Chili Crab

Chili crab is a dish of the hot south, where cooks believe spice is a form of hospitality. Tender crab meat, more hot spice than seems wise, and a pan so hot the kitchen coughs. The crab must be cooked in the spice, not under it, so stir it constantly. It is messy to eat and impossible to eat politely. I serve it with a warning and a large napkin, and I keep the napkin.

## 18238 Spotted Yellowtail

Yellowtail out of the Tanaris shallows, sold by a goblin who weighs his fish with one hand and the customer with the other. The fish is excellent anyway. It is firm and oily and needs only a hot pan and a single turn. Do not fuss with it, however much your hands want to. A spotted yellowtail is the rare fish that is better the less a cook does, and many cooks find that unbearable.

## 18240 Grilled Squid

Squid has two good cooking times, very short and very long, and everything between turns it to rubber. I prefer the short. Score the body in a crosshatch, rub in a little soothing spice, and put it over the fiercest grill in the house for as long as it takes to say its name twice. It curls, it chars at the edges, it is done. The cooks of the south give it an hour in a stew. They are wrong, but in an interesting way.

## 18242 Hot Smoked Bass

Hot smoking is smoking at a heat that also cooks, which is faster than cold smoking and less forgiving. Hot spice goes on the summer bass. Then the bass goes into the thick of the smoke, near enough to the coals to cook through in the time cold smoke would barely colour it. Stay with it. It is done when the flesh flakes at the thickest part, and soon after that it is dry. It is the only fish I would trust to a soldier's pack, and I have trusted it to several.

## 18244 Poached Sunscale Salmon

Poaching is the art of cooking in water that never quite boils, and it is the most neglected art in any kitchen. The sunscale salmon, from the warm seas, is rich and soft and would fall apart under a harsher method. Bring the water to a shiver, not a boil, lower the fish in and leave it. When it turns opaque, lift it out. The water should never once have raised its voice.

## 1250158 Clam Linguine

Linguine is a long flat noodle, and the recipe, to its credit, does not pretend to have one. It has zesty clam meat, cut in long strips to look the part, and turned through soothing spice. A hot pan, hardly any time, and straight to the table, because clam cooked a moment too long turns tough. On the plate the strips tangle like noodles and eat like clams. I approve of the deception.

## 18247 Baked Salmon

Whitescale salmon, a cold-river fish, baked whole with soothing spice in an oven that is warm rather than hot. A whole fish bakes evenly where a fillet dries at the edges, and the skin keeps the juice where it belongs. Slide a knife along the backbone: when the flesh lifts from the bone in one piece, the fish is done. The recipe is sold in Feralas, and I would make the journey for it, if Feralas were nearer.

## 18246 Mightfish Steak

The mightfish is the one fish I butcher as if it were beef, because it is cut into steaks and behaves like them. Hot spice and soothing spice, the first for vigour and the second for manners, pressed into both faces of the steak. A pan as hot as you dare, both sides seared dark, and then leave it on the board a while before the knife. The fishermen of the far south bring these in with nets and much shouting. I prefer them on a plate, in silence, as a steak ought to be eaten.

## 470370 Smoked Redgill

A recipe sold in Silithus, by the expedition's quartermasters, to soldiers who needed food that would keep in the sand. Redgill, smoked slowly until it is dry enough to travel and still tender enough to eat. No spice, only smoke. The soldiers who brought it back to Stormwind asked me to approve of it. I did, which surprised them more than the desert had.
